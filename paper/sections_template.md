## TITLE
Adversarial Robustness Is Not Zero-Day Generalization: A Controlled Evaluation of Flow-Based Intrusion Detectors on Corrected CIC-IDS2017

## Abstract
Synthetic variants of known attacks, including feature-space adversarial perturbations, are sometimes used as stand-ins for unseen ("zero-day") attacks when evaluating machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS). We test whether this proxy holds. On the corrected CIC-IDS2017 release we train Random Forest, XGBoost, a 1D-CNN and an FT-Transformer and compare, at a fixed 1% false-positive rate over five seeds, detection under constrained perturbations of known attacks (gradient-based and black-box attacks on 42 attacker-controllable features) with leave-one-attack-family-out (LOAO) detection, where an entire family is absent from training. {ABSTRACT_RESULTS} Robustness exceeds LOAO detection for every model ({GAP_STATS}), and the two measures agree at most on a coarse ranking of the models ({SPEARMAN_SHORT}). {MILP_ABS}Benign-only anomaly detectors{OS_ABS} do not close the gap.{TMP_ABS} On this benchmark and threat model, a robustness score should not be read as a zero-day score, and the two should be reported separately. Code and per-seed results are publicly available.
## Keywords
network intrusion detection, adversarial machine learning, zero-day attacks, generalization, evaluation methodology, CIC-IDS2017

## I. Introduction
Machine-learning NIDS routinely report near-perfect detection on public benchmarks, yet operational deployments face traffic and attacks that differ from the training distribution [@sommer], [@arp]. The question that matters in operation is not how well a detector separates known attacks from benign traffic, but what it does with an attack it has never seen. Labelled zero-day traffic is scarce by definition, so recent work approximates it, either by evaluating across datasets [@layeghy] or by synthesising "new" attacks from known ones, for example with generative models [@peppes], [@langat].

One synthesis route perturbs the feature vectors of known attacks with adversarial-example methods such as FGSM and PGD [@fgsm], [@pgd], or interpolates between attack classes. If a detector withstands such perturbations, it is tempting to conclude that it will also generalise to novel attacks. That inference is rarely tested. A perturbed flow stays close to the attack it came from; a genuinely new attack family may sit somewhere else entirely.

We did not plan to write this paper. Our original design used perturbed and mixed attacks as a synthetic zero-day test set, with held-out attack families added only as a sanity check that the synthetic set behaved like real novelty. It failed that check. Detectors that barely moved under perturbation missed most flows of a family they had never seen, and the mismatch became the subject of the study.

We therefore test the inference directly. On the same data, models and operating point, we compare robustness to constrained perturbations of known attacks with detection of attack families that are absent from training. We make three contributions:
- A controlled protocol that evaluates the same four detectors under both regimes on the corrected CIC-IDS2017 release [@engelen], [@liu22], after removing duplicate and label-conflicting flows, at a fixed 1% false-positive rate and over five seeds, with two unsupervised baselines as reference points.
- A threat model that restricts perturbations to attacker-controllable features, projects them back to the feasible feature range, audits their internal consistency, and includes a score-based black-box attack [@simba] and a tree-aware coordinate search, so that tree ensembles are attacked directly rather than through gradients they do not have [@kantchelian].
- Evidence that perturbation robustness overstates unseen-family detection by {GAP_LO} to {GAP_HI} percentage points and tracks it only in coarse ranking, together with practical reporting recommendations.

## II. Related Work
A. Generalization and evaluation pitfalls in ML-based NIDS
Sommer and Paxson explained early why machine learning struggles to find novel attacks in network traffic [@sommer], and Arp et al. catalogue pitfalls that inflate results in security ML, including data snooping and inappropriate baselines [@arp]. Cross-dataset evaluation exposes large performance drops when NIDS meet traffic from another network [@layeghy]. For unknown attacks, a systematic review surveys zero-day detection approaches [@ahmad], and Zoppi et al. compare supervised, unsupervised and meta-learning detectors on attacks that are unknown at training time [@zoppi]. Several recent works instead generate "zero-day-type" samples from known attack data, for instance with a GAN trained on known malware records [@peppes] or as synthetic attack signatures [@langat], and train or evaluate detectors on them, without testing whether such samples behave like genuinely new attack families. Generative models trained on known classes are likewise used to "synthesize samples of low-frequent and unknown attacks" [@yang20] or traffic that "mimics zero-day patterns" [@mu26], although the latter does evaluate with a leave-one-attack-type-out protocol. In adversarial-NIDS work, "zero-day" is sometimes applied to new evasion variants of known attacks, as when signatures of constrained attacks are proposed against "zero-day adversarial attacks" [@alhussien]; in this paper the term refers only to attack families absent from training. With a zero-shot protocol that holds out attack classes, Sarhan et al. found that, while most unseen classes are detected, detectors fail on certain attack groups whose features differ markedly from the training attacks [@sarhan], which is consistent with our LOAO results. The closest analogue outside security comes from computer vision, where Taori et al. found that robustness to synthetic image perturbations transfers little to natural distribution shifts across hundreds of models [@taori]; our study asks the same question for NIDS, with held-out attack families playing the role of the natural shift. Our LOAO protocol shares the goal of cross-dataset testing but holds the network environment fixed, isolating the novelty of the attack family, and it provides exactly the test that synthetic-proxy studies omit.

B. Dataset quality of CIC-IDS2017
CIC-IDS2017 [@sharafaldin] remains one of the most used NIDS benchmarks, but Engelen et al. documented systematic flow-construction and labelling errors and released corrected data [@engelen]; follow-up work quantified their prevalence across datasets and released improved versions [@liu22], and Lanvin et al. showed that such errors change reported detection performance substantially [@lanvin]. We use the corrected release and additionally remove exact duplicates and label-conflicting flows.

C. Adversarial attacks on NIDS
Gradient attacks [@fgsm], [@pgd] require a differentiable model; tree ensembles need dedicated evasion methods [@kantchelian] or black-box search [@simba]. Pierazzi et al. distinguish feature-space from problem-space attacks and formalise the constraints that realistic attacks must satisfy [@pierazzi], and Apruzzese et al. model realistic adversarial attacks against NIDS [@apruzzese]. Our perturbations are feature-space but constrained to attacker-controllable features; Section V-E measures how often they break flow-feature identities and repeats the experiment with those identities enforced.

D. Position of this work
Prior work typically measures either adversarial robustness or generalization to unknown attacks. To our knowledge, based on searches of OpenAlex and arXiv, no study measures both on identical models, data and operating point and tests whether one predicts the other; this is the gap we address.

## III. Methodology
A. Data and preprocessing
We use the improved CIC-IDS2017 release of Liu et al. [@liu22] (five capture days), which builds on the corrections of Engelen et al. [@engelen]. Following the dataset authors' recommendation, flows labelled "Attempted" are relabelled benign. Identifier and leakage-prone fields (flow ID, source and destination IP and port, timestamp) are dropped, leaving {N_FEATURES} numeric flow features after removing constant columns. Rows with missing or infinite values are removed first, leaving {N_RAW} flows. Exact duplicates (identical features and label) are then removed, and feature vectors that occur under more than one label are discarded as ambiguous; together these two steps remove {N_REMOVED} flows, leaving {N_FLOWS}. The label-conflict step uses labels from the whole dataset, but only to drop feature vectors that no detector could classify correctly under every label they carry, and it is applied identically before every split; Section VI gives its size. Attack labels are grouped into families: DoS, DDoS, PortScan, BruteForce (FTP/SSH-Patator), Botnet and WebAttack; Infiltration ({N_INFILTRATION} flows) and Heartbleed ({N_HEARTBLEED} flows) are too small for separate evaluation and are excluded from training and testing. Family sizes after cleaning are given in Table~\ref{tab:data}.

B. Sampling and splits
For each seed, every family is split 70/30 into disjoint train and test pools. From the pools we draw up to {BENIGN_TRAIN} benign training flows (20% of which form a benign validation set used only to set thresholds), {BENIGN_TEST} benign test flows, and up to {FAM_TRAIN} training and {FAM_TEST} test flows per attack family. All scaling statistics are fitted on the training sample only and then applied unchanged to validation and test flows. The split is random at the flow level, so flows from the same attack session can fall on both sides; Section V-A discusses what this does to the in-distribution numbers.

C. Detectors
We evaluate Random Forest [@rf] (100 trees, balanced class weights), XGBoost [@xgb] (300 trees, depth 8), a 1D-CNN (two convolutional layers with 32 and 64 filters, adaptive max-pooling and a 128-unit dense layer) and a compact FT-Transformer [@ftt] (32-dimensional feature tokens, two encoder layers, four heads). The choice is deliberate. Tree ensembles remain the strongest and most widely used option on tabular flow features, while the two neural models stand for the convolutional and attention-based designs most often proposed for NIDS; having both families matters here because, as the results show, they fail in different ways. Neural models are trained for {EPOCHS} epochs with Adam (learning rate $10^{-3}$) on signed-log, standardised features, with the positive class re-weighted for imbalance. All detectors are binary (benign vs. attack). The decision threshold of each model is set to the 99th percentile of its scores on the benign validation set (drawn from the training pool, never from test data), i.e. a target false-positive rate of 1%; all detection rates are reported at this operating point.

As unsupervised reference points we add an Isolation Forest (200 trees) and a fully connected autoencoder (encoder 64-16, mirrored decoder, 20 epochs, reconstruction error as score), both trained on the benign training flows only and thresholded in the same way. They never see an attack, so every family is unseen for them; they answer the question of whether held-out families are detectable at all in this feature space at a 1% alert budget. {OPENSET_METHOD}

D. Threat model and constrained perturbations
The attacker modifies flows of a known attack family to evade detection. Perturbations are applied only to the {N_MODIFIABLE} attacker-controllable features: forward-direction packet counts, sizes and timing, flow duration, inter-arrival and active/idle statistics, and flow-level size and rate aggregates. The rule we followed was simple: an attacker controls what its own host sends and when, but not how the victim answers or how the protocol stack frames the exchange. Backward-direction features (server responses), TCP flags, protocol fields and initial window sizes are therefore left unchanged. Perturbations are bounded in $L_\infty$ norm by $\epsilon \in \{0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5\}$ in the standardised signed-log feature space. In raw units, a step of $\epsilon$ multiplies $(1+|x|)$ of a modifiable feature by $\exp(\epsilon\,\sigma)$, where $\sigma$ is that feature's standard deviation in log space; {EPS_SCALE_TEXT} After each perturbation, samples are projected back to a feasible region: values are clipped to the training range, integer-valued features are rounded, and every min/mean/max triplet is re-ordered so that min $\le$ mean $\le$ max.

We use six perturbation procedures: (i) a random-sign control with the same budget; (ii) FGSM [@fgsm] and (iii) 10-step PGD [@pgd] computed on an independently trained MLP surrogate and transferred to every target; (iv) white-box PGD on the two neural targets; and (v) a score-based black-box coordinate search in the style of SimBA [@simba], which queries the target's score directly and therefore applies to tree ensembles. SimBA visits every modifiable feature in random order for two passes and keeps a $\pm\epsilon$ change whenever the attack score decreases; a five-pass variant is used as a convergence check (Section V-B). Because SimBA only tries the two corners of each coordinate's interval, it can step over the split points of a tree ensemble. We therefore add (vi) a tree-aware coordinate search for RF and XGBoost: for each modifiable feature it extracts the model's split thresholds that fall inside the flow's $\epsilon$-box, tries values just below and just above each of them (up to 24 per flow and feature) together with both box ends, and keeps the lowest-scoring one, for three passes. When at most 24 thresholds fall inside the box, this is exact one-dimensional minimisation of a piecewise-constant model along that coordinate; otherwise it searches a random subset of 24 of them.

E. Cross-family hybrids
As a second synthetic proxy, inspired by mixup [@mixup], we form convex combinations $\lambda x_a + (1-\lambda) x_b$ of test flows from two different attack families, with $\lambda \sim U(0.3, 0.7)$, projected with the same feasibility step.

F. Leave-one-attack-family-out (LOAO)
For each family $f$, all detectors are retrained on benign traffic and the remaining five families, the threshold is re-set on the same benign validation set, and detection of the unseen family $f$ is measured. LOAO preserves the network environment and feature extraction and changes only whether the attack family was seen, making it a controlled proxy for zero-day attacks.

G. Statistics
Every configuration is run with five seeds (0 to 4), which change the splits, samples and model initialisation. We report means with 95% Student-t confidence intervals across seeds. Changes in per-flow detection between clean and perturbed versions of the same flows are tested with an exact McNemar test [@dietterich], with Holm correction applied once across all {N_MCNEMAR} such tests (every model, procedure, budget and seed). We ask two separate questions about robustness and LOAO. The first is whether a robustness number overstates zero-day detection; both quantities are detection rates of attack flows at the same threshold, so we compare, per seed and model, the minimum detection rate over all attacks at $\epsilon \le 0.2$ (including the tree-aware search for the tree ensembles) with the mean LOAO detection rate. For brevity we call this minimum worst-case robustness; it is the worst case over the attacks we ran, not a certified bound, and a stronger attack could only lower it (Section VI). We also report a family-averaged variant that weights families as LOAO does. The comparison uses paired t-tests per model (Holm-adjusted across the four models, with the standardised effect size $d_z$) and a Wilcoxon signed-rank test over all model-seed pairs. The second is whether robustness predicts zero-day detection, i.e. whether more robust detectors also detect more unseen attacks; for this we use Spearman's rank correlation over the four model means, with an exact permutation test, because the model-seed pairs are not independent. Throughout, the primary metric is detection at the fixed 1% false-positive rate. AUROC averages over all thresholds, including ones no operator would use, so it can stay high while detection at a realistic alert budget is low; we report it only as a secondary metric and return to the difference in Section V-F.

## VI. Limitations
Our perturbations are feature-space. Enforcing the eight derived-feature identities (Section V-E) removes the most obvious inconsistencies, but it still does not guarantee that a real packet sequence exists for each perturbed flow [@pierazzi]; problem-space attack generation would be the stronger test. The attacker-controllable set also includes flow-level aggregates, such as packet-length statistics and Flow Bytes/s, that depend partly on the victim's responses; an attacker can move them only through its own packets. Restricting the attacker to forward-direction features alone would weaken the attack, which could only raise the robustness scores and widen the gap, so this choice is conservative with respect to our conclusion. {LIMIT_AMB}{LIMIT_SPLIT}A random split does not leak the held-out family into LOAO training, since that family is absent from training altogether. On the attack side, our black-box attacker sees continuous scores and may query freely, which is more than a deployed NIDS usually reveals, so the threat model errs towards a strong attacker; the remaining risk is on the search side: {LIMIT_MILP} Results come from one corrected dataset and binary detectors. WebAttack and Botnet test sets are small ({TE_WEB} and {TE_BOT} flows per seed) and their LOAO results vary strongly between seeds, so we do not draw family-level conclusions from them. Model capacity and training budgets are moderate, and stronger neural models may behave differently. Finally, we did not evaluate defences: adversarial training could raise the robustness side of the comparison, and domain-adaptation or open-set methods could raise the LOAO side. Whether either narrows the gap is an open and, in our view, the most useful next question.
